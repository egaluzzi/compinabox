**In one sentence:** the US Department of Justice (DOJ) has published guidance on how it evaluates corporate compliance programs, and a few of its themes shape what US buyers ask of their suppliers.

## Read this first

This is an overview of publicly known themes, not a summary of the document's text. We have not reproduced or quoted it, and we do not describe its details. If a buyer points you to the DOJ document, read the document itself, and ask a lawyer what it means for you.

The guidance is about how a US authority looks at companies' own programs. It is not a rulebook for suppliers in other countries, and no law of your country is changed by it.

## Why a non-US supplier hears about it

US companies and companies that sell to them want to show that their programs reach their suppliers. A buyer that borrows ideas from the DOJ's thinking may therefore turn them into questions for you. The result is that your customer's expectations can be shaped by it, even when you are far from the US.

## Three themes you will recognize

### Third-party due diligence

The DOJ's guidance pays attention to how a company looks at the third parties it works with. For a supplier, this can appear as a questionnaire about your company and your own vendors. A light version is a short questionnaire plus a screening against sanctions lists. A sanctions screening is a first filter, and it is not an investigation.

### Ongoing monitoring

A program is not something you write once. Buyers tend to ask whether anything keeps happening after the documents exist: reminders, refreshed training, new employees receiving the rules, concerns being reviewed.

### Documented recordkeeping

The guidance emphasizes records that tie things together: training, use of the reporting hotline, and signed acknowledgments. In plain terms, it is not enough to say you train people; you need to show who did it and when.

## What this means in practice

If you can show a code of conduct, policies, a reporting channel, training and a record of acknowledgments, you are answering the questions that these themes tend to produce. That does not mean you satisfy the DOJ's guidance or any audit. It means you have the documents that buyers commonly ask about.

## A note on tone

Do not treat these themes as a checklist to be satisfied once. Buyers who ask about them are usually trying to understand whether your company takes the subject seriously in daily work. Short, honest answers, supported by dated records, tend to land better than long statements. If you cannot show something yet, say so and explain when you will. That is a better position than describing a process that does not exist.

## What to do next

1. Ask your buyer which documents or standards they refer to, instead of guessing.
2. Check which of the three themes you can already show with records.
3. Decide how you will keep records current, for example on a yearly cycle.
4. Have a lawyer review anything that matters for a contract.
