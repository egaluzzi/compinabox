**In one sentence:** large US and European buyers often ask their Latin American suppliers to show a documented compliance program, even when local law does not ask for one, and size does not exempt you.

## Why the request arrives

Two things push smaller companies to prove they have a compliance program: bidding on a government contract, and selling to a large multinational customer that audits its suppliers. This article is about the second one. Neither cares how big you are. Both want documentation.

For the buyer, the supplier is part of its own risk. Many multinationals therefore send questionnaires, ask for documents, or schedule audits before and after signing a contract. The pressure comes from the buyer's own expectations, and does not always depend on a local law.

## What buyers commonly ask for

The requests vary by buyer, but the same items come up again and again:

- a **code of conduct** that carries your company's name;
- **written policies** on bribery, gifts and hospitality, conflicts of interest and dealing with public officials;
- a **reporting channel** that employees and partners can use, ideally with a confidential or anonymous option;
- **training**, with a record of who completed it;
- a **record of acknowledgments**, showing who read and accepted the policies;
- some evidence that you **look at your own suppliers**, for example by screening them against sanctions lists.

## Why records matter as much as documents

A policy that nobody has read is weak evidence. Buyers tend to look for a trail that ties things together: the policy, the people who accepted it, the training they took, and the way concerns are reported. A short, consistent set of records is usually better received than a long document with no trace of use. The next article in this series covers a US source of these expectations.

## Answering a questionnaire well

Questionnaires are easier when the answers already exist in writing. Answer the question that was asked, point to the document that supports it, and keep the dates visible. If a question does not apply to your business, say why instead of leaving it blank. Keep a copy of what you sent, because the next buyer will probably ask something similar.

## Local laws exist too

Latin American countries have their own rules, and they differ. In Brazil, for instance, the Clean Company Act (Lei 12.846/2013) uses the existence of an effective program as a mitigating factor when a company's liability is examined. We do not summarize other countries' rules here, and nothing in this article tells you what applies to your company. A local lawyer can.

## What to do next

1. Collect the questionnaires and audit requests you have received, and note what they ask for.
2. List which of the items above you already have in writing.
3. Fill the gaps before the next request, not after it.
4. Keep the records in one place so you can show them quickly.
5. Ask a lawyer about the rules that apply to your sector and your contracts.
